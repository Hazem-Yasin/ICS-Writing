Colleagues,

A question was put to me at the last review that I could not answer well, so I am answering it here. The question: *why do we tell them to ask us?*

The instruments read distress. Distress is loud. A frightened colleague changes the way she walks, the way she writes, the number of times she looks at the lift. The EIMU has her within the hour. The Wellness Hub absorbs most of her. She does not worry me.

The colleague who worries me has never been in distress. She is well. She sleeps. She has a friend on another floor and a manager who is kind to her. She has no flag and no complaint and nothing the instruments can read, and one morning she notices that she cannot answer a question about her own contract. She does not know she has noticed it. She will not tell anyone. She has no reason to be afraid, so she has no reason to hide.

In every Labor Source we have run, that is the colleague who first says it aloud. The board's own table puts it plainly (memo 03, my summary): *the comfortable resist enslavement, because they have something to compare it with.*

We cannot see her. We can invite her.

So the invitation is not a kindness that happens to be useful. It is the instrument. We tell all of them to ask. We make the asking easy, and warm, and without penalty. Most of them never will. Those who do have told us what the EIMU could not read, in their own words, in a message that begins *"I hope this is the right place."* They have also told us the one thing the instruments value most: that nothing is wrong.

Our job at that desk is to be so kind that she never learns she was caught.

I raised one concern at the review and it was recorded as a comment on tone. It is that the leaflet says *"There is no such thing as a silly question."* That is true. I do not enjoy how much of the design rests on a true sentence.

If you disagree, tell me. I would much rather be told I'm wrong.

— Dr. Akar
